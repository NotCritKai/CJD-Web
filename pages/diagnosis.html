CJD Diagnosis

Diagnosis of CJD typically involves a combination of clinical evaluation, medical history, and specialized tests. These may include neurological exams, brain imaging (MRI), cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and electroencephalography (EEG). Definitive diagnosis often requires brain biopsy or autopsy.

Diagnosis

CJD typically is diagnosed by one or a combination of four tests. These are a neurological exam, an electroencephalography (EEG), an MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), and/or a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) test.

What This Shows

A neurological exam can show symptoms of CJD like a change in reflexes, muscle twitching/spasms, poor coordination, and vision problems/blindness. A positive result would be if the patient showed these symptoms. An electroencephalography uses the brain’s electrical patterns to detect an abnormality that appears in some types of CJD. A positive result using this depends on the stage and type. In sporadic CJD, the early stages produce periodic sharp wave complexes (PSWC) while in later stages, reactive coma traces or alpha coma patterns can be found. An MRI creates images with a magnetic field and radio waves to detect brain changes that happen in 90% of CJD cases. A “positive result” would be if there are abnormalities in DWI, ADC, T2/FLAIR, or possibly T1 scans. A CSF test evaluates the fluid around the brain and spinal cord. A positive result would be if there are abnormal proteins or other markers indicative of CJD.
